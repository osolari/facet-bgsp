\abstract{%
Block generalized-power methods are attractive because they combine threshold-based structural selection with inexpensive Stiefel updates, but the historical block formulation conflates two nonequivalent objectives and leaves inactive components and post-selection refitting ambiguous. We reconstruct the model from first principles. The normalized loading-ball problem reduces exactly to a sum of soft-thresholded Euclidean norms, whereas the widely used squared-threshold objective admits a different quadratic-lift primal. We prove both identities, exhibit a two-component counterexample separating their maximizers, and specify valid zero-response, threshold-tie, rank-deficiency, and mask-before-refit policies. For the squared model we distinguish the historical full-polar iteration from a safeguarded Riemannian ascent method. We prove monotonicity of the full-polar step and establish whole-sequence convergence of the safeguarded method via a sufficient-ascent lemma, a relative-error lemma, and the Kurdyka--\L{}ojasiewicz property, which holds because the soft-threshold objective and the Stiefel constraint are semi-algebraic. Conditional on convergence, strict threshold margins imply finite support and sign identification. After identification, the hard-threshold rank-one case reduces to classical power iteration, while heterogeneous block supports require a coupled polar-map Jacobian rather than a single common covariance operator. We derive these local maps, a locally safe screening principle, and precise failure cases at zero margin or repeated active eigenvalues. Controlled experiments on planted spikes validate the predicted identification and local-rate relations (including a two-branch validation that separates pure power-iteration contraction from the shifted contraction introduced by the soft-threshold gradient). Modern-AI experiments extend the pipeline to a GPT-2 small residual stream, a vision-transformer CLS stream, and the Jeffers Pitprops classical sparse-PCA benchmark; the thresholded block-sparse components match dense PCA on reconstruction, exceed it on monosemanticity and class-conditional specialisation, and avoid the reconstruction cost that a sparse-autoencoder dictionary of matching width incurs for its $\ell_1$ sparsity. Plugging a certified safe-screen mask into the solver via a dedicated screened operator produces machine-precision agreement with the full solve while realizing the full $(n{-}k)/n$ oracle-cost ceiling.}
